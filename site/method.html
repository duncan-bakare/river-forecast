Method and limitations

What this is, and what it is not

  • This is a personal, experimental research project. It is not an official flood warning service and not a flood forecasting service. Nobody has checked it for fitness for any purpose, and it is provided without warranty.
  • It can be wrong, including badly wrong in a flood. It can also be late, or switch itself off when its data are missing or old.
  • Do not use it to decide whether to protect property, move vehicles, travel or evacuate. Use the official sources: floodinfo.ie (OPW), met.ie/warnings (Met Éireann), your local authority, and the emergency services (112 or 999).
  • It says nothing about whether any property or road will flood. It forecasts one number: the water level at one gauge.

What it forecasts

The hourly mean water level of the River Suir at the Office of Public Works (OPW) gauge at Clonmel (station 16011), in metres above Ordnance Datum as OPW publishes it, 6, 12, 24 and 48 hours after the latest hour for which all inputs exist. Each forecast comes with a 90% interval. For comparison it shows two simple alternatives: persistence (the level stays where it is) and a linear baseline.

The two dashed levels on the chart (16.70 m and 16.91 m) are statistical values published by OPW for this gauge: the level exceeded 1% of the time, and the median annual maximum. They are not flood-warning levels and do not mark the start of flooding.

Reading the times

All data are in UTC. Irish time is shown alongside: GMT in winter and Irish Standard Time (IST, UTC+1) in summer. A forecast "for 20:00" is the mean level over the hour starting at 20:00. "+6 h" counts from the latest data hour, not from the moment you look. OPW's hourly rainfall arrives late, so the latest data hour (an hour-start label) is usually two to three hours behind the clock, and a "+6 h" forecast is then only about three to four hours ahead of you.

Data

  • River levels: OPW's real-time 15-minute levels for Clonmel and 14 gauges upstream on the Suir and its tributaries (updated hourly here), with the OPW Hydro-Data archive (Clonmel from 1953) used to build and test the model. Recent data are provisional: OPW has not yet checked them, and they can be revised.
  • Rainfall: 24 OPW rain gauges in and near the catchment (hourly totals). Almost all of this rainfall record is unchecked by OPW. We screen it ourselves (spikes, and gauges that disagree with their neighbours or drift), which removed 22 gauge-years and corrected one gauge that read about twice as much as its neighbours since 2019.
  • Weather warnings: Met Éireann's public warnings feed, shown as published for Tipperary, Waterford, Kilkenny, Limerick and Cork. They are not an input to the forecast.
  • No rainfall forecast is used. See the limitations below.

The model

  1. LightGBM (gradient-boosted trees), one model per lead time, predicting the change in level from now. Inputs: recent changes at Clonmel and upstream, the mean of the screened rain gauges over 1 hour to 30 days, and the time of year.
  2. 90% intervals from quantile models, widened by conformal calibration on 2019–2021 and then recalibrated on the eight biggest floods (see limitations).
  3. Training and testing use time only: trained on data to the end of 2018, tuned on 2019–2021, tested on 2022 onwards. The eight biggest floods since 2006 were removed from training and used as separate tests. Training and calibration use only OPW-checked levels.
  4. Everything is re-computed every hour. If the latest reading is older than three hours, an input is missing, or the forecast file is older than three hours, the site shows no forecast. If many inputs are missing it says so.

Known limitations

  1. No rainfall forecast. The model only knows rain that has already fallen. Beyond about 12 hours most of the skill depends on future rain, which it cannot see. At 24 hours its advantage over persistence is much smaller in floods (about a third less squared error), and at 48 hours it is not reliably better in floods, and worse than persistence on the 2026 unchecked data. A model given perfect future rain would do far better, so the gap is the missing forecast. Met Éireann's forecasts are being archived so this can be tried later.
  2. The intervals are too narrow in floods. The 90% intervals contained the true level in about 86–88% of hours in everyday conditions, but only 65–81% of hours in the eight biggest floods, even after recalibrating. Errors are largest while the river is rising and in the days before a flood arrives.
  3. Few floods. Only eight large floods are available for testing, and five of them are earlier than the training cut-off, so they are held-out events, not tests on the future. The results are uncertain and probably optimistic. No test on genuinely new data has been done yet.
  4. No peak-size accuracy. At 48 hours the model underestimates the peak in all eight past floods (by 0.1 to 0.5 m).
  5. Provisional and unchecked data. Live inputs are unchecked, so a faulty gauge or rain gauge can distort a forecast. The checks above catch only some faults.
  6. One gauge. Nothing here reflects flood defences, river works, weirs or reservoirs, blockages, tides in the lower Suir, or changes to the channel since the training data were collected.
  7. Data lag. The forecast is as old as its inputs (see "Reading the times"). A sudden rise in the last two hours is not in it.
  8. No guarantee of availability. It runs on a small automated system without monitoring staff and may stop without notice.
